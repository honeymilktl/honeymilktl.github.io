Translation Notes

  1. ↑ Adonis first explains TPO in English and then reiterates it in Japanese. To keep things simple, I decided to alter this line a bit.
  2. ↑ Koga refers to the term 風見鶏 here, which can mean both a weather vane and an opportunist or fence-sitter. To reflect Adonis' confusion, I chose to tweak the line a bit while incorporating some bird-related imagery.
  3. ↑ Adonis seems a bit insecure here, as the phrase "血相を変える" is tricky. It suggests a change in complexion but doesn't clarify the emotion without an accompanying action.
  4. ↑ Kaoru is using the English word for 'Easter' here, so I opted for another name in the previous instance that resonates more with the event and the meaning of the holiday itself ( aka. the resurrection of Jesus Christ ).
  5. ↑ Rei's most likely making a comparison between his family's ritual and the observance of Passover. During the Spring Harvest Festival the first sheaf of newly cut barley is offered up as a sacrifice. There are also other rituals reminiscent of ancient pagan practices in which male lambs are kept and then sacrificed on the eve.
  6. ↑ Kaoru's using the term 御神酒, aka. omiki, a sake that's used in Shinto purification rituals. It's a bit similar to sacramental wine ( or grape juice ) used during the Christian Eucharist.
  7. ↑ Rei uses an interesting term here, aka. 浮世 which has many connotations: a fleeting life, this world and the next, a lamentable, melancholy world, etc. The Buddhist concept of the word is no longer used, but texts during the Heian period in regards to the sorrowful world would state things like, " because life is short and painful, it is best to float along and enjoy it." This coincides pretty well with Rei's mindset where he jokes while being simultaneously serious.
  8. ↑ A genre of linked verse poems that contains forms like haiku and senryū. One of the most famous poets of the Edo period, Matsuo Bashou, made it his life's work to transform this form of poetry into a literary genre to describe the mundane.
  9. ↑ The ringed staff of a Buddhist priest that's typically used either in prayer or as a weapon.
  10. ↑ A quote from Natsuhiko Kyogoku's novel The Summer of Ubume, in which the protagonist basically asserts that there is no supernatural, just things we don't yet understand. It's a fancy way for Rei to imply he has no ulterior motives.
